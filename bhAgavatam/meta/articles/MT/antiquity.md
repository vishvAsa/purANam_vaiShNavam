Even a novice with some itihAsa-purAna background will note that the bhAgavata is linguistically rather unique. If you have spent some time with the mahAbhArata, harivaMsha, and the viShNupurANa, this distinctness will be even more apparent. It will become clear that while it belongs to their tradition in the broad sense, it has been crafted in a very different way – it is *not* a foundationally sauta text.+++(5)+++ 

Hence, E. Bryant has a point that it is an unparalleled project. It is also obvious to anyone who has studied the shruti that the author(s) were intimately acquainted with not just the vedic text but also shrauta praxis. There were such brAhmaNa-s with a vaiShNava bent or affiliation in South India. So it is tempting to claim it was composed _de novo_ in South India due to the praise of southern devotees. 

From textual and inscriptional evidence, such vaiShNava-oriented V_1s were common in the North, too, prior to the Islamic cataclysm. Hence, at best, I believe that it underwent a late redaction in South India. Despite its strongly unified appearance, I suspect two phases of its composition. 

An earlier veda-aligned phase by vaiShNava-oriented $V_1s$ engaged in shrauta praxis with an acceptance of vaiShNava-bhakti in household practice. While where and when it happened is harder to place, we cannot rule out the North here. My inclination is to date it to the gupta age. 

This was followed by a second South Indian phase in a largely bhakti-oriented community. I suspect at least the $V_1s$ of these communities might have had some long-term continuity, even if they had migrated from the north to the south at some point.

I guess the question how "largely" do they ignore it. But let's say they don't see it is a foundational text. It is indeed for reasons like that one cannot blindly accept a simple version of the South Indian theory.
