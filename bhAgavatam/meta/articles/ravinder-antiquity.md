One mishearing in a Tamil song, one Pallava temple, and a fight over when the Bhāgavata Purāṇa was written.

## Hardy's guess
In a song of Periyāḻvār, a ninth century Tamil poet, the child Kṛṣṇa reaches for shining butter. In the Bhāgavata Purāṇa the same child's jewelled body becomes a lamp in a dark house. Friedhelm Hardy thought the second line was born from the first, by ear.

> "The Prabandham [the Āḻvār hymns] has as the object of the verb oḷi veṇṇey 'shining butter'; assuming now that the BhP [the Bhāgavata] understood it as *oḷi veṇ mey 'shining white body', we can explain BhP X, 8, 30 svāṅgaṁ dhṛta-maṇi-gaṇam artha-pradīpam '(making) his own body which wears many jewels the lamp for his senses (=eyes)'. I think this is a good example to show that a) the BhP borrowed from PālTM [Periyāḻvār's Tirumoḻi], and b) that the BhP knew of the songs only orally (*veṇ mey instead of veṇṇey). However, the question will require further study, which cannot be undertaken here."

That hedge is Hardy's own, and so is the asterisk, his mark for a reconstructed phrase. If Hardy is right, the most influential Kṛṣṇa book in Sanskrit was made by a man who carried the Tamil songs in his ear. 

Hardy read the Bhāgavata as a translation

Hardy's book, Viraha-Bhakti, came out in 1983 and runs to seven hundred pages. The book's core argument is that emotional Kṛṣṇa devotion is a southern invention and that the Bhāgavata carried it into Sanskrit. His twelfth appendix prints about fifty numbered parallels between the two bodies of poetry, and number 24 sets a flute stanza in each language side by side. First the Bhāgavata's Sanskrit, then Periyāḻvār's Tamil.

> "When Mukunda, his left cheek resting on his left shoulder, his brows moving playfully, plays the flute resting on his lips, while his tender fingers press the stops."

"When Govinda, his left cheek resting on his left shoulder, his brows arching and moving upwards, his broad hands joining together [around it] and the corners of his mouth enclosing it, plays the flute."

Direction is the harder question, and Hardy argues it in a numbered list of his own.

"But a few remarks about the direction of influence may be made, although I believe it is fairly obvious by now that the BhP utilizes the Alvars, and not vice versa. (1) While the Alvars are not familiar with the Br/ViP version [the older telling in the Harivaṁśa and Viṣṇu Purāṇa], the BhP uses it as its major narrative source. (2) Separate incidents (e.g. TPa [Āṇṭāḷ's Tiruppāvai], NTM I and III [her Nācciyār Tirumoḻi]) are woven together to form one narrative episode. (3) TVM II, 1 [a song of Nammāḻvār], when turned into Sanskrit (BhP X, 90), is exploited but additions are made so that a full song, complying with the demands of the Skt. ālaṅkārikas [the Sanskrit literary theorists], emerges. (4) When describing Krsna's bullfight [by which he wins his bride], the whole setting is changed to one comprehensible to a non-Tamil audience; the parallel to the Kalittokai [a classical Tamil anthology] is best explained as borrowing by the BhP."

"(5) The whole rearrangement of chapters 21 to 29 rests on the TPa v. 1 [the Tiruppāvai's opening verse], as the model. (6) None of the songs for which we have found parallels in the Alvars, can in any way be derived from the Br/ViP version. (7) When introducing the messenger song 47, 12-21, the BhP considers it necessary to explain to a non-Tamil audience 'imagining it to be a messenger sent by Krsna' (v. 11). (8) In the rearrangement of chapters 21-9, and in 33, 8 and 11, are left traces of the old mullai rainy season symbolism [a Tamil landscape convention of lovers' waiting] which cannot be derived from the ViP [Viṣṇu Purāṇa]."

"We could go on adding further reasons, but there is no need to keep on stating the obvious."

Hardy's own summation, quoted there, is that the Bhāgavata is trying to hold four things together at once, a northern social order and a northern philosophy against a southern religion of feeling and the aesthetic inheritance of old Tamil caṅkam poetry.

> "The BhP tries to integrate all four complexes, and it uses the symbol of the Vedas to achieve this, while adopting the puranic literary form. Thus, as authors have time and again pointed out, the BhP stands quite apart from other puranas."

The butter at the top was his last brick, and a Tamil word nearby, cotikkiṉṟaṉ, shows the same ear at work. On the larger find Hardy drops his hedge.

> "BhP X, 22 is an extremely interesting, because so rare, example of a specific mythologeme which can be traced back from its puranic appearance to its roots in oral and vernacular sources."

His conclusion puts the book on a map.

> "Somewhere in the Pāṇṭiya nāṭu, not later than the ninth or early tenth century A.D., the BhP was composed on the basis of the ViP as its puranic model and orally current Āḻvār songs."

His date rests on three poets he could not date firmly

If the Bhāgavata quotes Periyāḻvār and Āṇṭāḷ, it is younger than they are, and Hardy puts both in the ninth century. The far wall is Bryant's to describe.

"The Muslim traveler Alberuni referred to the 'Vasudeva Bhagavata' (i.e., the Krsna Bhagavata), as part of a list read to him from the Visnu Purana itself in 1030 CE... Alberuni's date appears to set a definite upper limit for the text that is slightly earlier than Ramanuja in the twelfth century."

Inside that window Hardy picks his floor.

"It is quite unlikely that these Alvars, except Nammalvar, lived before the eighth century A.D. The ninth century would be more likely. Thus the ninth or earlier tenth century will appear as the most reasonable date for the BhP."

His own word for his Āḻvār chronology is fragile, and on the three poets carrying the weight he says it outright.

"Unfortunately my dating of the first three of these Alvars rests on less firm grounds than one would wish."

He marked the weak floorboard himself.

## Edwin Bryant & temple
Edwin Bryant thinks a temple at Kāñcīpuram rules it out. The Āḻvārs, Tamil poets of the Vaiṣṇava south, sang viraha, love in separation as a way of standing before god, and the question is which came first, their songs or the Sanskrit.

Bryant, who ends up rejecting nearly all of this, states it fairly before he goes to work on it.

> "Hardy finds that emotional bhakti surfaces for the first time with the Āḻvārs, and contrasts it with the more intellectual bhakti of the North. He holds that the confluence of the two streams in the South between the 6th and 10th century C.E. resulted in the creation of the Bhāgavata. By using Vedic archaisms, the text attempted to relate itself to the most powerful symbol with which northerners had been challenging southern culture: the Vedas."

A temple finished around 770 already has the story on its walls

Bryant jumped there, in an article of 2002 built on Dennis Hudson's study of the Vaikuṇṭha Perumāḷ temple at Kāñcīpuram, whose sculpted panels Hudson read as running through Kṛṣṇa's story in the Bhāgavata's own order.

> "This temple was completed in circa 770 C.E. by the Pallava emperor Nandivarman II Pallavamalla. The sequence of the panels situated in the temple specifically follows that of the episodes of the Bhāgavata Purāṇa. While each of these episodes may occur individually in other Purāṇas, the fact that these panels follow the specific sequence of the episodes in the Bhāgavata precludes the possibility that the craftsmen were using some other textual source for the narratives they were engraving. The text, then, must have preceded the date when the temple embodying its narratives was completed, in the 8th century C.E."

Single episodes float around the purāṇas freely. An order is a fingerprint, and a temple of about 770 cannot be quoting poets of the ninth century.

Bryant then turns the borrowing around, reviving a suggestion the French Indologist Jean Filliozat had made back in 1962.

> "However, this temple embodied Bhāgavata narratives in the 8th century, and thus predated these particular Āḻvārs. One is thus forced to take seriously Filliozat's consideration that it could well have been the Āḻvars who borrowed from the Bhāgavata rather than vice versa."

He adds a second lever, which is patronage. A text does not get carved onto a royal foundation the year it is written.

> "One would have reasonable grounds to suppose that the Bhāgavata must have been in existence for at least two or three centuries before it could generate the necessary royal patronage for the construction of several temples depicting its narratives by the 8th century. Clearly, the Vaikuntha Perumal Temple precedes the South Indian Vaisnava teachers noted by van Buitenen and any project of sanskritization in which they may have been engaged."

## Archaism
Nobody can explain the archaic Sanskrit

The Bhāgavata is stiff with Vedic forms that had been dead for a thousand years, and it is alone in its genre in this. The ruling explanation comes from the Sanskritist J. A. B. van Buitenen, and Hardy adopts it with a southern edge: the archaism is strategy.

> "[I]n the archaism of the Bhagavata we have the expression of the same concern. The Krishna legend has to sound Vedic because it was Vedic... [T]he high sounding language... gave appropriate notice of its Vedic orthodoxy."

Bryant's objection is that nobody else in the history of the language ever did this, or tried.

> "One would have to note that there are no other examples in Sanskrit literature involving this type of extensive calculated manipulation of archaic language forms. Van Buitenen's explanation would rest on more secure footing if there were other examples of this type of strategy on this scale."

Van Buitenen had conceded as much himself, calling it a unique phenomenon. 

Hudson, whose temple gave Bryant his date, goes further than Bryant is willing to.

> "I do not think it likely that archaic Sanskrit was used sometime between 800 and 1000 C.E. to make a new text appear old, but instead that genuinely ancient materials gathered texts around them as the Bhāgavata religion developed over many centuries."

Hudson would put the Kṛṣṇa books before the Viṣṇu Purāṇa. Bryant records that, declines to follow, and stops at the archaism itself.

> "All these indications point in the direction of the Gupta period as the latest probable date at which the final, complete version of the Bhāgavata Purāṇa could have been written, and to the North of the subcontinent as a possible, perhaps probable, locus of its composition."

Gupta period means the fourth to sixth century. Between the two men: four hundred years, fifteen hundred kilometres.

The two arguments never quite meet

## Tamil origin?
Bryant meets the bullfight, the contest by which Kṛṣṇa wins his bride, which Hardy had called an exclusively southern event, head on.

"Southern sources borrowed northern motifs extensively, and one would have to produce compelling evidence as to why the reverse could not be the case on occasion."

The Tamil rhyme schemes in the gopī songs he grants and then disarms.

"[The Bhāgavata] uses more different poetic meters than almost any other text in the history of Sanskrit literature... [its author] might have had access to southern poetic forms without necessarily being a native of the South himself."

The substratum words get a footnote.

> "Hardy (1983) also gives three instances of obscure word usages in the Bhāgavata, which point to a possible Dravidian linguistic substratum, but these are not convincing to my eye, and far too insignificant a number to prove his case."

The best strike is the geography. Ray, writing in 1932, had argued that the Bhāgavata knows Vraj, Kṛṣṇa's own country, only vaguely, and the south well.

> "Ray's contention that the awareness of the land of Vraj seems vague is even more applicable to Balarāma's journey to the South, where numerous places of pilgrimage are counted off as if by rote in twenty-odd verses, with a few principal features noted very curtly, as if from hearsay. No southern reference gives any indication of prolonged familiarity, so the argument works both ways."

That blow lands. And yet the fifty parallels go undiscussed, the eight arguments go undiscussed, and the butter appears nowhere in Bryant's article. I searched his full text for it.

The hole runs the other way too. Everything hangs on Hudson's reading of the panels, Hudson's own datings run to 700 before the common era, where even Bryant will not follow, and John Stratton Hawley, who edited the very volume Bryant published in, doubts the parallel case from the Cāḷukya temples at Paṭṭadkal. Nobody has retested the sequence.

## What would settle it?

My own reading. Hardy holds the strongest evidence and Bryant the strongest object, and an object beats an inference when they collide. Both men can be right, but only if the one assumption they share is wrong: one book, one date. A purāṇa is not a manuscript, and nobody says so better than Bryant.

> "Much of the endless conjecture and differences of opinion amongst scholars results from the fallacy of assigning old dates to an entire text on the basis of an archaic reference, which might just be an ancient well preserved fragment in a later compilation, or of assigning a much later date to an entire text on the basis of a more recent dateable reference such as a dynasty of the historical period, which might be a much later interpolation in an older text."

Which cuts at his own conclusion as much as at Hardy's. An old Kṛṣṇa core with real Vedic language in it, early enough for a Pallava emperor to build around, then reworked in the Tamil country by a man with the songs in his ears, satisfies the stone and the butter at once. What would move the question is not another argument but the wall itself: the Vaikuṇṭha Perumāḷ panels reread against the Bhāgavata and the Viṣṇu Purāṇa, and the sequence published. Everything here rests on one reading of it, made thirty years ago.
